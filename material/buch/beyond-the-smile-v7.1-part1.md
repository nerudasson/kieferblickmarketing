**The system you were never taught:**

Here is the argument I want to make — and I know it is a bold one.

Two specialties have existed side by side for decades with a clear distinction between them: aligner orthodontics and the prevention and treatment of temporomandibular disorders. Aligner clinicians move teeth. TMD clinicians manage joints and muscles. The two rarely speak to each other in the same clinical workflow.

I believe that distinction is not just artificial — it is actively harming patients.

Aligners are uniquely positioned to be the most powerful, non-invasive long-term solution we have for preventing and treating TMD. No other tool in dentistry gives you the ability to reposition the teeth, guide the mandible to a new postural position, and do it incrementally, reversibly, and over a period of months. When that tool is applied with functional principles — when the teeth are moved not from maximum intercuspation toward cosmetic improvement, but toward a therapeutically stable position that the joints and muscles can sustain — the results are categorically different from conventional aligner treatment.

That is a strong claim. I hope that by the time you finish this book, you will find it not just convincing but obvious.

*Section 1: The system and its three components*

The masticatory system is an integrated functional unit. Teeth create the contact points that guide mandibular movement. Muscles generate the forces and position the mandible. Joints are the pivots around which all movement occurs. No component functions independently. Change one and you change the load carried by all others.

The clinical consequence: a tooth movement is never just a tooth movement. It is a change to the contact geometry of the entire system — and the muscles and joints respond to that change whether you planned for them to or not.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 1.1: "The Three Components" — simple three-part diagram. Three interconnected circles labelled TEETH, MUSCLES, JOINTS. Bidirectional arrows between all three. One arrow highlighted red showing TOOTH MOVEMENT → MUSCLE RESPONSE → JOINT LOADING. Clean, minimal. Caption: "The masticatory system is one machine with three components. You cannot change one without the others responding."]*

*Section 2: The view from above — what the lateral diagram doesn't tell you*

In university, we almost always saw the TMJ from the side. The lateral cross-section is instructive — condyle, disk, eminence, perfectly illustrated. But it is quietly misleading, because it suggests that the condyle is a simple cylinder rotating on one axis.

Look at the joint from above, and the picture is completely different.

The two condylar heads, viewed from the top, are angled inward relative to the horizontal plane. They are not parallel — they converge toward the midline at roughly 15–20 degrees each. The condylar heads and the posterior teeth form a triangle when viewed from above — and this same triangular shape repeats in the architecture of the condylar heads themselves and in the shape of the fossae they rotate in.

The clinical consequence of this angulation is crucial: because the condyles are angled inward, the only anatomically stable rotation point during opening is the **medial pole** of each condyle. The lateral poles must translate as the jaw opens, but the medial poles can serve as a genuine rotation point if the condyles are correctly seated. This is why centric relation is defined around the most superoanterior position of the condyle — specifically, the seating of the condyle against the posterior slope of the articular eminence at the medial pole. The architecture is designed for this.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 1.2: "The View From Above" — two-panel diagram. Left: standard lateral cross-section as seen in textbooks. Right: superior view of the mandibular condyles and posterior dental arches, showing the triangular convergence of the condylar heads toward the midline, medial poles highlighted. Caption: "The lateral view is accurate. The superior view is the one that explains centric relation."]*

*Section 3: The disc — not a cap, but a bucket handle*

The articular disc is almost universally drawn as a flat oval cap sitting on top of the condyle. That image is so pervasive that most clinicians carry it as their mental model. It is wrong.

The disc is biconcave in its central zone — thinner in the middle than at its anterior and posterior poles. And it is not simply circular when viewed from the front: it is shaped more like a bucket handle, curving around the condyle's medial and lateral poles rather than sitting flat on top. This geometry means the disc is simultaneously a load-bearing structure in the central zone and a guide structure at its periphery — the thick posterior band acts as a posterior stop, the thin central zone bears the compressive load, and the anterior band connects to the superior head of the lateral pterygoid.

The clinical consequence: when the lateral pterygoid fires out of sequence, it pulls the disc anteriorly. The thick posterior band — the disc's posterior stop — slides over the condyle's lateral pole as the joint loads. That is the mechanical basis of disk displacement with reduction. It is not random. It is a predictable consequence of disrupted disc-condyle coordination.

*Section 4: Why muscles always win*

The most useful sentence in functional occlusion is this one: *muscles always win.*

When there is a conflict between where the teeth want the jaw to be and where the muscles want the jaw to be, the muscles win. Every time. The teeth will wear, the joints will remodel, the disc will displace — but the muscles will continue to bring the mandible to the position they have learned to reach, regardless of what the occlusion does to them in the process.

The clinical consequence: if you plan aligner treatment from maximum intercuspation and the muscles have been guarding to compensate for a CR-habitual discrepancy, you are planning from the muscles' compensation. The treatment will end where the compensation began.

*Section 5: The interference chain — from bad contact to jaw pain*

A single deflecting occlusal contact is not just an occlusal problem. It triggers a cascade. The contact deflects the mandible. The elevator muscles — specifically the masseter and medial pterygoid — respond to the deflection by contracting asymmetrically to maintain contact. Asymmetric contraction sustained over time produces uneven joint loading. Uneven joint loading disrupts disc position. Disc displacement with the system at load produces clicking, and then pain.

The chain from a small occlusal interference to jaw pain is not random. It has a mechanism, a sequence, and a clinical fingerprint at each stage.

Brief one-line description under each box. Red highlight on the first box to show where the chain begins. Caption: "A single deflecting contact doesn't stay local. The masticatory system has no firewall between a tooth problem and a joint problem."]*

> **THE SITUATION:** 34-year-old woman. No TMD history. Seeking aligner treatment for mild crowding. Two weeks after fitting aligner trays: unilateral jaw fatigue and mild temporal headache. No joint sounds.
>
> **THE FINDING:** A single aligner tray contact on the lower right second premolar — 40 microns thick, confirmed with shimstock — producing a deflecting contact at closure. The masseter on the right was hyperactive on palpation.

*Section 1: The real problem with conventional aligner planning*

Conventional aligner treatment typically begins from one of two starting points: maximum intercuspation, or a position derived primarily from external facial photographs and aesthetic landmarks. Both have legitimate uses. Neither, on its own, tells you where the condyles are comfortable. Neither tells you whether the bite you are building will be one the joints and muscles can sustain over five years.

This is the gap. Not a gap in technique — a gap in starting point.

The goal of functional aligner therapy is to move the teeth to a position that is therapeutically stable: one where the condyles are correctly loaded, where the muscles can function without compensation, and where the result is built to last rather than built to look good at debond.

Three major schools of thought — Dawson/Pankey, Slavicek/Vienna, and Roth — have each developed frameworks for identifying and capturing this position. They use different names and different techniques. But when you strip away the vocabulary, they are all describing the same anatomical reality.

The brief version: Dawson calls it centric relation. Slavicek calls it the reference position. Roth calls it power centric. When deprogramming quality is controlled, the 3D condylar positional differences between techniques are clinically insignificant — under 0.2mm in well-controlled studies. The debate was always more about vocabulary than anatomy.

> *[SIDEBAR BOX: "Where the Schools Genuinely Disagree — and Why It Doesn't Stop You"]*
>
> *The convergence on the anatomical target is real. So is the divergence on how to get there. Three specific differences are worth naming honestly:*
>
> *First, the role of instrumentation. The Vienna School places significant emphasis on condylography — electronic recording of condylar movement — as a verification tool before registration. The Dawson tradition relies primarily on manual technique, load test, and reproducibility testing. Both approaches produce valid results for the majority of patients; the question is how complex the case is and how much verification you need.*
>
> *Second, the meaning of "fully seated." Dawson's bimanual technique applies gentle operator-directed guidance to achieve full condylar seating. Slavicek's chin-point approach allows the patient's own musculature to find the terminal border position — the operator provides direction but not force. In a well-deprogrammed patient, both arrive at the same position. In a patient who is tense, anxious, or guarding, the difference in approach can produce a meaningful difference in result.*
>
> *Third, the vertical dimension. Roth's power centric includes a specific posterior tooth relationship at the registered position; the other schools leave vertical dimension as a separate clinical decision.*
>
> *This book teaches both the Dawson and Slavicek pathways — not because they are interchangeable in every situation, but because the skill to adapt your technique to the patient in front of you is more valuable than loyalty to a single school. Chapter 6 covers both in clinical detail.*

*Section 3: The six principles all schools agree on*

This is the heart of the chapter. Six shared principles that define what the therapeutic position actually is — stated in plain language, with no school-specific terminology:

1. **A reproducible reference is non-negotiable.** You cannot plan treatment without a consistent, verifiable jaw position.
2. **Condyle-disc integrity is the prerequisite.** All schools require the disc to be properly positioned before the reference position is meaningful.
3. **Deprogramming is essential.** Separating the teeth to release neuromuscular programming is universal across all three schools.
4. **The position must be verified, not assumed.** Reproducibility testing and patient comfort are universal checkpoints.
5. **TMJ health takes precedence.** No school advocates forcing joints into positions they cannot accept.
6. **The occlusion must harmonise with this position.** Any slide from first contact to full closure is a functional problem.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 2.2: "The Six Shared Principles" — a clean visual list, each principle in a bordered box. The six boxes arranged in two columns of three. A brief icon for each. Caption: "Despite different vocabularies, all three major schools converge on these six principles."]*

*Section 4: The therapeutic position — a working definition for aligner clinicians*

The term *therapeutic position* functions as a school-neutral label. It is defined on three levels simultaneously:

- *Pragmatic:* the position that lets you plan treatment with a reproducible reference
- *Biological:* the position the joints are healthiest in — or most comfortably adapted to
- *Clinical:* the position you can capture, verify, and plan from consistently

For aligner treatment specifically: the floating digital scan needs to be anchored to this position before it is clinically meaningful. A habitual occlusion registration captures compensation, not anatomy. A registration derived from facial photographs alone captures aesthetics, not function. The therapeutic position is the correct starting point for every functional treatment decision that follows.

*Section 5: Healthy joints vs. adapted joints*

Not every patient presents with a healthy reference position. Dawson described "adapted centric posture" — the stable, asymptomatic position a joint has remodelled to after adaptation. Slavicek described the equivalent. The clinical implication is important: registering an adapted position is not a technique failure. It is a diagnostic finding. The chapter closes on this distinction because it is what separates a functional clinician from a dogmatic one — the willingness to work with what the joint can do, rather than insisting on what the textbook says it should do.

*Forward reference:* How to actually capture this position — which technique, what deprogramming protocol, which material, and which verification system for which patient — is the subject of Chapter 6.

### Chapter 3: The TMJ as Adaptive Organ — How Far Can It Go?

**Core Message:** *The masticatory system can adapt to an enormous range of loads and positions. For most patients, it absorbs the stress of orthodontic treatment without any sign of distress. Understanding which patient is in front of you changes everything about how you proceed.*

**Common Misconception:**

> *"If the patient has no symptoms and no history of TMD, aligner treatment is functionally low-risk."*

The purpose of the Functional Quick Check in Chapter 4 is to find the load the history doesn't report.

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: The adaptive biology of the TMJ*

The temporomandibular joint is one of the most adaptable structures in the body. Its articular surfaces are covered in fibrous tissue — not hyaline cartilage — which makes them unusually responsive to load changes. Apply consistent loading within the joint's adaptive range, and the bone and fibrocartilage remodel to accommodate it. This is normal biology. It is why people can wear orthodontic appliances for two years and emerge with joints that function perfectly.

The adaptive capacity has a limit. Load the joint beyond what the tissue can remodel to — or load it asymmetrically, or intermittently at high intensity — and the adaptation becomes pathological. This is the mechanism behind a substantial proportion of TMD cases — and a significant proportion of treatment-related symptom onset.

*Section 2: The predisposing factors — what loads the system before you begin*

The masticatory system does not arrive at a treatment consultation as a blank slate. It carries the history of everything it has already been through. The clinically important predisposing factors:

- **Parafunction** — bruxism, clenching, and other habitual loading patterns that generate sustained or repetitive joint force beyond functional chewing loads
- **Previous occlusal changes** — previous restorations, previous orthodontic treatment, prosthetic work that altered the bite; every occlusal change makes a demand on the system's adaptive resources
- **Postural factors** — forward head posture and cervical mechanics influence mandibular position and masticatory muscle tension; the jaw does not work in isolation from the rest of the body
- **Systemic factors** — inflammatory joint disease, hormonal factors (joint laxity is more prevalent in women of reproductive age), and psychological stress all reduce the joint's tolerance for additional loading
- **History of trauma** — even minor trauma that appeared to resolve completely may have left the disc-condyle relationship subtly altered

None of these factors is a contraindication to aligner treatment in isolation. Together — or in combination with a treatment plan that adds further load from a poor starting position — they can tip a system that was coping into one that is not.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 3.2: "The Predisposing Factor Stack" — a visual "weight stack" diagram. On one side: the patient's adaptive capacity. On the other: the accumulating load of predisposing factors. A tipping point is shown. Caption: "It is not any single factor but the cumulative load that matters."]*

*Section 3: Orthodontic treatment as a contributing factor*

This is an uncomfortable truth that the orthodontic profession has been reluctant to discuss openly: orthodontic treatment — including aligner treatment — is a contributing factor to TMD in susceptible patients *(Level 1 evidence: Michelotti & Iodice, 2010; McNamara et al., 1995)*.

It is not, in most patients, a problem. For patients with adequate adaptive capacity and a treatment plan that respects the therapeutic position, aligner treatment is well-tolerated and the joint adapts without incident. But for patients whose adaptive capacity is already partially or fully consumed before treatment begins, adding the load of 12–24 months of incremental tooth movement can tip the system from coping to symptomatic.

*Section 4: Three patients, three positions on the spectrum*

Patients A, B, and C — who will accompany you through this entire book — represent three positions on the adaptive capacity spectrum.

**Patient A** (31-year-old woman, mild crowding, no symptoms) sits clearly within her adaptive range. Her system has reserve. She will absorb the load of aligner treatment without incident — as long as that treatment is planned from a therapeutically stable position.

**Patient B** (44-year-old man, moderate crowding, mild masseter tenderness) sits closer to the centre of the spectrum. His system is coping — but its reserve is reduced. Parafunction and previous dental work have already consumed capacity. He is the patient where the clinical decision at Chapter 4 matters most.

**Patient C** (38-year-old woman, active disk displacement, chronic jaw fatigue) has already exceeded her adaptive capacity in the areas of joint loading and disc position. She is not a candidate for aligner treatment today. But she is a candidate for the process that ends with aligner treatment — and Chapter 5 describes the network that makes that process possible.

*Section 5: Functional aligners as both prevention and treatment*

The argument closes with a reframe. Functional aligner therapy is not just a safer way to do conventional aligner treatment. It is an active therapeutic intervention for the masticatory system.

For Patient B, planned correctly, it prevents the dysfunction that was approaching. For Patient C, planned correctly after pretreatment, it is the final stage of a therapeutic sequence that restores function to a system that had lost it. The aligner is not the decoration at the end of the treatment — it is the instrument that moves the teeth to the position the entire preceding work was designed to establish.

*Section 2: The Functional Quick Check — three domains, one answer*

The Functional Quick Check covers three domains:

**History** — five questions that reveal whether the system has been under strain: jaw pain or fatigue, temporal or morning headaches, joint sounds (clicking, grinding, grating), episodes of limited opening, history of trauma or previous TMD treatment. Each positive answer is a signal, not a diagnosis.

**Clinical examination** — palpation of the masseters (tension and tenderness), temporalis (anterior and middle fibres), and condylar poles bilaterally (tenderness at rest); range of motion (opening >40mm normal, lateral excursion >8mm bilaterally normal); deviation on opening (>2mm warrants attention).

**Functional assessment** — occlusal contact quality at closure (bilateral simultaneous posterior contacts, absence of deflecting contacts) and a joint loading test.

The Quick Check is fast because it is focused. It is not trying to characterise the type of disk displacement or classify the patient's bruxism. It is trying to answer: *does this system have the reserve capacity to absorb aligner treatment, or does something need to be addressed first?*

## PART III: CAPTURING THE CORRECT BITE

### Part Introduction — Mini-Manifesto

*The bite registration is not a technical formality that precedes the real work. It is the pivot point of the entire functional aligner workflow.*

*A scan taken from the right position tells the software where to move the teeth. A scan taken from the wrong position tells the software to move the teeth from a compensation — planned with extraordinary precision to the wrong destination.*

*For Group B patients, the bite registration happens once, with care, and is verified before treatment begins through the prealigner. For Group C patients, the registration follows months of pretreatment — and it is the moment at which the therapeutic work done by the network becomes the starting point for the aligner plan.*

*The single chapter in this section covers both pathways: what makes a registration valid, how to execute each pathway step by step, and how the prealigner serves as the final verification before active treatment begins.*

---

### Chapter 6: Capturing the Bite — Two Pathways, One Goal

**Core Message:** *The bite registration is the most consequential step in the functional aligner workflow — and the approach is different depending on which patient group is in your chair. For Group B, a well-executed manual registration with a digital facebow is the standard. For Group C, dynamic verification with electronic jaw tracking adds precision and patient communication that no other tool provides.*

**Position in the four-act structure:** Act III, Chapter 6. Emotional target: competence. *"I now know exactly what to do at this step — and why it is different for different patients."*

**Callback line:** *In Chapter 4, every patient group was assigned a roadmap. Group B leads to bite registration with Axioprisa. Group C leads to bite registration with dynamic verification. This chapter explains both pathways in full — and connects them to the prealigner that follows.*

**Recurring Patients:** Patient B (Group B pathway) and Patient C (Group C pathway) are the central clinical illustrations.

---

**Common Misconception:**

> *"The bite registration is a technical step — once you know the technique, it's straightforward."*
>
> The technique is the easy part. The variable that determines whether a registration is valid is the neuromuscular state of the patient at the moment of closure — and that cannot be controlled by technique alone. A tense patient, a rushed appointment, or a patient who "helps" by actively closing into the material will produce a registration that looks technically perfect and is clinically invalid. The neuromuscular state must be managed before the technique begins. That is what deprogramming is for — and it is what most clinicians skip.

---

**Chapter Opener Vignette:**

Two patients. Two appointments on the same day. Both here for bite registrations.

Patient B: 44 years old, risk factors present, no active symptoms. You have deprogrammed him with a Lucia jig for fifteen minutes. He reports the jaw feels neutral. You are ready to register.

Patient C: 38 years old, four months of successful pretreatment behind her. Splint therapy resolved her active symptoms. She is in your chair for the first time in a new jaw position — one her body has been building toward for months. She has been in pain for two years. She needs to know that what she has been through has led somewhere real.

*The same step. A completely different moment. This chapter covers both.*

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: What makes a registration valid — five criteria*

A bite registration is clinically usable if it meets all five:

1. **Captures the therapeutic position** — condyles are fully seated at the moment of closure; the registration records anatomy, not compensation
2. **Reproducible** — the procedure repeated produces the same result within 1mm
3. **Dimensionally stable** — the material does not distort on removal or in transit
4. **Appropriate thickness** — posterior separation of approximately 2–3mm; too thin and teeth contact through the material; too thick and vertical dimension is artificially altered
5. **Definitive indexing** — the registration seats on models or digital scans without rocking or ambiguity

Criterion 1 is the most commonly unverified. Criterion 2 is most commonly skipped because of time pressure. Both depend on one thing: the neuromuscular state of the patient at the moment of closure.

A tense patient will close to their habitual compensation, not to the therapeutic position. The solution — for both Group B and Group C patients — is deprogramming. The technique differs between groups; the principle is identical.

*Section 2: The Group B registration pathway*

**Who:** Patients with risk factors but no active TMD. Muscles may be slightly tense. The system is coping, but adaptive reserve is reduced.

**Deprogramming:** Lucia jig or leaf gauge, 15–20 minutes. Patient should report the jaw feeling "neutral" or "loose" before proceeding.

**Manual guidance — two options:**

The Dawson bimanual approach — bilateral finger contact on the inferior border, gentle superior-anterior direction — is highly teachable and well-validated. The Slavicek chin-point approach — single contact at the mental protuberance, patient-guided closure — is preferred in the Vienna School for patients where operator guidance may introduce artefact. Both reach the same anatomical target when deprogramming quality is adequate. Chapter 2's school neutrality principle applies here: match the technique to the patient and your training, not to tribal loyalty.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 6.1: "Two Manual Techniques — One Target" — two-panel lateral diagram. Left: Dawson bimanual — hand position, direction of gentle superior-anterior force. Right: Slavicek chin-point — single finger at mental protuberance, gentle upward direction. Caption: "Two routes to the therapeutic position. The technique matters less than the neuromuscular state when the registration is taken."]*

**Verification:** Take two registrations in sequence. They should agree within 1mm. If they differ more: deprogramming was incomplete. Re-deprogram, repeat.

**The digital facebow:** Without facebow data, the virtual articulator uses an average hinge axis position. For any case with meaningful vertical dimension changes, significant CR-habitual discrepancy, bimaxillary involvement, or functional finishing goals — which describes most functional aligner cases — the average is not adequate. The Axioprisa (or Axioquick) locates the individual transverse hinge axis and records the spatial relationship of the maxillary arch to that axis. This data anchors the digital scan to the patient's actual anatomy rather than a population average.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 6.2: "What the Facebow Adds" — two virtual articulator views side by side. Left: scan mounted without facebow — based on average hinge axis. Right: same scan mounted with facebow data — correctly oriented. Caption: "A scan without a facebow reference is like a building plan without orientation to north. The measurements may be perfect. The building still faces the wrong way."]*

**Group B step sequence:**
1. Functional Quick Check → Group B classification
2. Deprogramming (Lucia jig/leaf gauge, 15–20 min)
3. Manual bite registration (bimanual or chin-point)
4. Verification — repeat registration, agree within 1mm
5. Digital facebow recording (Axioprisa/Axioquick)
6. Case documentation: scans in max intercuspation + scans in therapeutic bite + X-ray + intra/extraoral photographs
7. Prealigner fabricated in therapeutic bite position
8. Prealigner wear (2–4 weeks) → comfort verified → proceed

> *[ILLUSTRATION 6.3: "Group B Registration Pathway" — horizontal workflow strip, eight numbered steps with icons. Caption: "The Group B pathway from classification to treatment start."]*

*Section 3: The Group C registration pathway*

**Who:** Patients with active TMD symptoms, post-pretreatment. The joint has been stabilised through splint therapy and specialist support. The registration appointment is where the therapeutic work done by the network becomes the starting point for the aligner plan.

**Why Group C is different — and why it matters to the patient:**

For Group C patients, the registration is not just a clinical procedure. It is a moment of proof. This patient has been in pain. She has worn a splint, attended physiotherapy, invested time and trust on the promise that this process leads somewhere. The registration appointment is where that promise becomes concrete.

A static registration confirms where the condyles are when the patient closes. For Group C patients, that is necessary but not sufficient. You want to know more: how the condyles move through the opening and closing arc, whether there is symmetry, whether the arc of closure is consistent across multiple repetitions. This is what electronic jaw tracking systems provide.

**Dynamic verification — Zebris, Modjaw, and DMD:**

These systems record three-dimensional mandibular movement electronically — not just a static bite position but the arc of closure, the envelope of movement, and the reproducibility of the terminal arc over multiple closures. For Group C patients this data serves two purposes:

*Clinically:* It verifies that the registered position is genuinely therapeutic — condyles arriving reproducibly, arc symmetric, no compensatory deviation during closure. It also informs treatment planning: the arc of closure affects how the aligner sequence is designed in the final stages.

*For the patient:* Patient C can see the recording. After two years of jaw fatigue and headaches, being shown a clean, symmetric arc of closure — and being told this is the position her aligner treatment will move her teeth toward — is not just clinically informative. It is deeply reassuring. It makes the treatment real in a way that bite registration material cannot.

Handling these systems in full detail — setup, calibration, data interpretation — is outside the scope of this book. What matters here is when to use them and why. For Group C patients: always. For Group B patients: an option in complex cases.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 6.4: "Dynamic Jaw Tracking — What It Shows" — two-panel graphic. Left: schematic of sensor setup. Right: example arc of closure recording showing condylar path both sides, terminal position marked, reproducibility across three closures shown. Caption: "Electronic jaw tracking records the movement, not just the position. For Group C patients, the arc of closure is as important as where it ends."]*

**Group C step sequence:**
1. Functional Quick Check → Group C classification → pretreatment phase
2. Pretreatment: splint therapy ± specialist involvement (Chapter 5 network)
3. Pretreatment endpoint confirmed: symptoms resolved, system stable
4. Dynamic bite registration: electronic jaw tracking → arc of closure recorded → therapeutic position verified
5. Manual registration taken at verified position
6. Case documentation: scans in max intercuspation + scans in therapeutic bite + X-ray + intra/extraoral photographs
7. Prealigner fabricated in therapeutic bite position
8. Prealigner wear (2–4 weeks) → comfort verified → proceed; if not comfortable → repeat registration

> *[ILLUSTRATION 6.5: "Group C Registration Pathway" — horizontal workflow strip parallel in format to Group B. Caption: "The Group C pathway from pretreatment completion to treatment start."]*

*Section 4: The prealigner — the final verification*

Before attachments are placed, before a single active aligner is delivered, every functional aligner case — Group B and Group C alike — goes through one step most conventional workflows omit: the prealigner.

The prealigner is a static aligner fabricated in the therapeutic bite position. It does not move any teeth. It holds the jaw in the registered position and asks: is the patient comfortable here?

If comfortable: proceed. Place attachments, begin active aligner treatment.
If not comfortable: do not proceed. Repeat the registration.

The prealigner is not a delay. It is the cheapest insurance policy in the entire workflow.

> *[CALLOUT BOX: "The Prealigner in 30 Seconds" — What: a static aligner in the therapeutic bite position. Why: confirms patient comfort before active treatment begins. Duration: 2–4 weeks. Decision: comfortable → proceed; not comfortable → repeat registration. Full coverage in the treatment section.]*

*Section 5: Case documentation — what belongs in the file*

Regardless of group, complete documentation before active treatment includes: scans in maximum intercuspation, scans in therapeutic bite position, bite registration material or digital record, facebow data file (Group B) or dynamic jaw tracking report (Group C), intraoral and extraoral photographs, X-ray diagnostics, prealigner comfort verification note. This is the clinical baseline against which every mid-treatment check and the final result will be compared.
