## PART IV: FROM REGISTRATION TO TREATMENT PLAN

### Part Introduction — Mini-Manifesto

*You have done everything the conventional workflow does not. You have screened the patient. You have deprogrammed, registered, and verified the therapeutic position. You have anchored the scans to the skull. You are now holding something that conventional aligner planning has never had: a valid functional starting point.*

*Here is what the conventional workflow does with that starting point: it ignores it. Not deliberately. Not through negligence. But because aligner software, by default, plans from maximum intercuspation and positions scans approximately in space — and unless the clinician explicitly overrides both defaults, the registration they just spent twenty minutes taking will have no effect on the treatment plan that follows.*

*This section begins with the chapter that names that divergence directly — the moment where functional and conventional aligner planning go their separate ways. It then builds through the protocols that make the functional approach work across complex cases and long treatments: phasing, distalization, and the systematic use of the functional baseline the registration established.*

*By the end of Part IV, the registration is no longer a procedural step. It is the foundation of a fundamentally different kind of plan.*

### Chapter 9: Two Different Games — The Planning Dance Between Clinician and Technician

**Core Message:** *The moment the bite registration is done, the real work begins — not in the software, but in the conversation between clinician and technician. Functional aligner planning is a different game from conventional treatment, and the outcome depends on how well that conversation goes.*

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: Three decisions at the treatment chair*

The wax bite is done. The scans are taken. Before anything leaves the practice, three questions need answers.

**The first question is how to close the scans.** The lower arch scan was taken in the therapeutic position — mandible held in the registered CR, wax bite separating the arches posteriorly. The planning software needs to know how to relate the upper and lower arches at the start of the setup. The correct default is to close the scans to first contact along the arc of closure. The wax bite provides the jaw relationship — the CR reference — not the vertical opening.

**The second question is where the teeth are going.** Before the plan goes to the lab, the clinician needs a rough roadmap in mind for each quadrant. Where are the molars going — distal, mesial, neither? What is the target for the anterior segment? Is the goal Angle Class I, or is there a defensible reason to plan a compromise? Most clinicians send this information as an afterthought. The functional approach treats it as the core of the brief.

**The third question is what to do about the midlines.** The clinician was in the room with the patient. The clinician saw the face, assessed the skeletal midline, noted the dental midline discrepancy. Unless it is written down and sent to the lab, the technician will be guessing. The midline decision is one of the most clinically significant choices in a setup. Specify it. Write it down. Send it with the scans.

Arrows show the arc of closure path. Caption: "The wax bite captures the jaw relationship, not the vertical dimension. Closing to first contact along the arc of closure is the correct default in almost every case."]*

*Section 2: The difference — what you are actually doing differently*

In conventional aligner treatment, the patient closes naturally into the scanner. Maximum intercuspation becomes the jaw reference. The scans are placed in approximate spatial orientation in the software. The result is often excellent — for patients with healthy joints and minimal CR-habitual discrepancy, it is clinically adequate. But it has a structural blind spot: the condyles are never referenced.

In functional aligner planning, the CR registration sets the jaw position. The facebow or CBCT data anchors the scans to the skull. The treatment planner now works from a verified jaw relationship — a position the joints can sustain — and the planned finish is designed to meet the teeth there, rather than asking the joints to accommodate wherever the teeth land.

Patient B is the clearest illustration of this. His CR registration placed the mandible 3mm forward of his habitual position. The apparent Class II tendency that drove his original 36-stage plan reduced by 1.5mm at the therapeutic position. The molar correction the original plan was designed to achieve was, to a significant degree, the correction of a compensated jaw relationship. When the registration was correct, the case needed 28 stages, a different occlusal goal, and a completely different approach.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 9.2: "Two Starting Points" — split diagram. Left: conventional workflow — scan in habitual occlusion, condylar position unknown, MIP as goal. Right: functional workflow — CR registration integrated, scans skull-anchored via facebow, therapeutic position as goal, condylar position verified. Same patient, same crowding. Different foundation entirely. Caption: "The difference is not in the software. It is in the starting point the software is given."]*

*Section 3: The planning dance*

Here is the truth that most aligner training courses do not address: the quality of a functional aligner case depends on the quality of the conversation between clinician and technician. Not primarily on the software. On the conversation.

There is a predictable failure mode in this relationship. The clinician sends the scans and assumes the technician will know what to do. The technician receives the scans, the registration, and a brief that says "align crowding, improve aesthetics." He is missing almost everything he needs to produce a functional plan — the midline specification, the occlusal roadmap, the quadrant-by-quadrant goals. But he builds the case anyway, because asking feels like admitting uncertainty. He makes his best guess. He sends the plan. The clinician approves it. Six months later, something is not right.

This failure mode is preventable. The tool that prevents it is not software. It is communication.

> *[INSIGHT BOX: "The Brief That Actually Works"*
>
> *A functional setup brief should contain, at minimum:*
> - *Jaw position reference: "CR registration integrated — please mount from the registration file, not the habitual scan"*
> - *Scan closure instruction: "Close to first contact along the arc of closure"*
> - *Midline specification: "Upper dental midline is 1mm right of facial midline — maintain this. Lower midline to upper."*
> - *Occlusal goals by quadrant: "Distalize upper right molar 2mm. Maintain upper left. Lower arch — relieve crowding in place."*
> - *Finishing goal: "Angle Class I preferred; will accept Class I right/I-II left if joint-stable."*
> - *Clinical context: "Amber patient, monitored throughout — flag any interceptor geometry issues before approval."*
>
> *This takes five minutes to write. It eliminates most of the guesswork that produces setup failures.]*

*Section 4: The functional setup review — five questions before approval*

When the setup arrives, these five questions determine whether it goes back or gets approved:

1. Is the jaw position set from the CR registration, not habitual occlusion?
2. Does the planned final occlusion have bilateral simultaneous posterior contacts at the therapeutic position?
3. Is anterior guidance present and functioning in the planned final setup?
4. Has the occlusal plane been maintained, or deliberately changed with clinical justification?
5. Are there posterior excursive interferences detected by the virtual articulator?

If any question cannot be answered confidently, send the plan back. Not as a failure — as the second half of the planning dance.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 9.3: "The Setup Approval — Five-Question Decision Tree" — vertical flowchart. Each of the five questions is a decision node. At each: answer "Yes / Confirmed" → continue down. Answer "No / Uncertain" → branch right to action: "Send back with specific query." Base of tree: "All five confirmed → Approve." Caption: "Five questions before any functional setup is approved. Each one is a structural check the conventional approval process does not include."]*

### Chapter 10: Case Phasing — When One Plan Is Not Enough

**Core Message:** *Some cases cannot be planned from a single functional baseline. The mandibular position changes too significantly during treatment to make the original registration valid for the full arc of tooth movement. Phasing is not a complication — it is precision applied to moving targets.*

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: The Class II division 2 prototype*

The Class II division 2 patient presents with retroclined upper incisors — often noticeably upright or even lingually inclined. The habitual closed position looks like a Class II with a deep bite. The condyles are seated in the glenoid fossa, but the mandible is trapped — the lingually inclined upper front teeth act as a stop, limiting anterior translation.

The CR registration captures this position. And it is valid — it accurately describes the jaw relationship as it exists at the start of treatment.

Phase 1 uprights the upper incisors. As they tip forward, the anterior stop is removed. The mandible is free to translate anteriorly. By the end of Phase 1, the jaw relationship that the original CR registration described has changed. The condyles may seat differently. The mandible's habitual closure path is different. The therapeutic position has shifted — typically forward and sometimes inferiorly.

Planning Phase 2 from the Phase 1 registration is now planning from a description of a jaw relationship that no longer exists. The case needs a new registration before Phase 2 begins.

Caption: "Uprighting the upper incisors releases the mandible. The jaw relationship captured in the original registration no longer exists. Phase 2 needs a new baseline."]*

*Section 2: The other indications for phasing*

Two other clinical situations reliably indicate planned phasing.

**Large CR-habitual discrepancies.** When the discrepancy between habitual closure and the CR registration exceeds approximately 2–3mm, the jaw relationship will shift meaningfully during treatment as the occlusion adapts to the new functional plan. Treating the full case as a single phase assumes the discrepancy remains stable — an assumption that large discrepancies do not support.

**Pronounced precontacts.** When the CR registration reveals a heavy first contact on a single tooth — typically a posterior premature contact — the teeth redistribute load as the functional plan progresses. The original precontact resolves, the arch adapts, and the midterm jaw relationship is meaningfully different from the starting position.

In both situations, planning the full case from the initial registration means asking the second phase to correct toward a goal that was designed for a different jaw.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 10.2: "When to Phase — Three Clinical Triggers" — three-panel graphic. Each panel shows the clinical scenario (Class II div 2 retroclined incisors / large CR-habitual discrepancy / pronounced precontact), the change that occurs during Phase 1, and a Yes/No indicator for new registration. Visual style: simple annotated diagrams, not photographs. Caption: "These three scenarios are not complications. They are predictable. Plan the phase transition before treatment starts."]*

*Section 3: The phase transition clinical checkpoint*

The phase transition is not a surprise event — it is a planned clinical checkpoint. Before Phase 2 begins:

- **Load test:** Has the TMJ response changed since baseline?
- **Muscle palpation:** Any new tenderness?
- **Patient report:** How does the bite feel? Any morning symptoms?
- **Shimstock check:** Bilateral contacts at the therapeutic position?
- **Visual comparison:** Does the mandibular position at the planned phase endpoint match the original CR registration, or has it shifted?

If all findings are consistent with the start of treatment, the original registration is still valid. One line in the notes. The next phase proceeds.

If any finding has changed — new tenderness, a changed condylar position on palpation, patient-reported bite changes — a new registration is indicated. Fifteen minutes at the chair, before the Phase 2 brief goes to the lab. Not a setback. The precision tool that makes a complex case predictable.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 10.3: "The Phase Transition Decision Tree" — vertical flowchart. Entry: Phase 1 complete. Five checkpoint branches (Load test / Palpation / Patient report / Shimstock / CR comparison). Each: "Unchanged from baseline" → proceed. "Changed" → new registration required. Exit branch for "proceed": plan Phase 2 from existing registration. Exit branch for "new registration": retake CR → new brief → new phase. Caption: "Five questions at the phase transition. Most of the time, all five confirm the original registration is still valid."]*

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: The first unfamiliar thing — occlusal interceptors*

The small structures on the posterior occlusal surfaces are occlusal interceptors. They are the mechanism that makes the whole plan work.

When the patient closes into the aligner, the opposing arch contacts the interceptors bilaterally. The posterior teeth behind the interceptors are separated from the opposing arch — not loading each other. The mandible is held in a defined relationship to the maxilla, dictated by the interceptor geometry, regardless of where the teeth are at that particular stage of treatment.

Think of them as the aligner equivalent of a precisely engineered splint — but one that changes geometry with every new aligner, always maintaining the therapeutic reference while the teeth move around it. Every stage. Same jaw relationship. Different tooth positions. The interceptors are what bridges those two requirements simultaneously.

They are not accidental. They are not decorative. They were designed by the technician based on the CR registration and the brief you sent. If they look unfamiliar, that is because conventional aligner plans do not have them.

Panel 2: same stage with interceptors — structures on posterior surfaces, opposing arch contacting them bilaterally, posterior teeth visibly discluded behind. Caption: "Occlusal interceptors hold the mandible in the planned therapeutic position at every stage of treatment. They are a dynamic positioning system built into the aligner itself."]*

*Section 2: The second unfamiliar thing — the disclusion*

When the posterior teeth are discluded — separated from the opposing arch, not bearing occlusal load — the movement environment changes fundamentally.

In conventional aligner treatment, posterior teeth are under occlusal load throughout. Every tooth movement occurs against a background of closing forces transmitted through the aligner. This resistance limits movements that require clean, unloaded force — particularly molar distalization, torque correction, and vertical changes.

With posterior disclusion through interceptors, that counterforce disappears. The posterior teeth move through unloaded space. The aligner force is the dominant force on the tooth. Movement becomes more predictable, more complete, and achievable in fewer stages.

The analogy: imagine pushing a heavy piece of furniture across a carpeted floor. Now imagine someone sits on it while you push. The same applied force produces a radically different result. Disclusion is what lifts the weight off the furniture.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 11.2: "The Movement Environment — Loaded vs. Discluded" — two-panel diagram. Left: conventional aligner stage — occlusal load arrows on posterior teeth, movement arrows resisted. Right: functional aligner stage — interceptors in contact, posterior teeth discluded, movement arrows unopposed. Force magnitudes shown. Caption: "Disclusion removes the counterforce that limits molar distalization, torque correction, and vertical movement in conventional aligner mechanics."]*

*Section 3: The rules — when interceptor geometry is wrong*

Interceptors are not arbitrary bumps. Their geometry has firm requirements, and when those requirements are violated, the interceptor stops being a mandibular stop and starts being a ramp.

**Bilateral symmetry:** The interceptors must be equal in height and contacted simultaneously. If one side contacts before the other, the mandible deflects toward the lower side. Unilateral interceptors are, in almost all cases, a planning error.

**Inclination limits:** An interceptor surface that is too steeply inclined relative to the arc of closure will generate a horizontal force component — pushing the mandible laterally or anteroposteriorly rather than holding it in place. The inclination must be within approximately 15–20° of perpendicular to the closure arc. Beyond this, the interceptor generates a destabilising horizontal push. A competent technician reviews interceptor geometry across the full sequence, not stage by stage in isolation.

**Sequential adaptation:** The interceptors change with every aligner stage. A geometry error at any single stage propagates forward. This is why a setup review that focuses on the final tooth position and ignores the interceptor sequence through the middle stages will miss the most common functional planning errors.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 11.3: "Interceptor Inclination — Stable vs. Unstable" — two-panel diagram. Left: correct inclination — closure arc perpendicular to interceptor surface, vertical load only, no horizontal deflection. Right: excessive inclination — closure arc at angle to surface, horizontal force component generated, mandible deflecting. Force arrows shown. Caption: "An interceptor surface that is too steeply inclined stops being a stop and starts being a ramp."]*

*Section 4: The third unfamiliar thing — distalization in the first stages*

In most functional aligner cases, distalization is the opening move. Planned deliberately. Executed first. Before any significant crowding relief at the front.

There are two reasons for this sequence. First, the mechanics: the molars are moving through a discluded, unloaded environment in the first phase, which is when distalization is most efficient. Second, the spatial logic: distalization creates space at the back of the arch before crowding relief is attempted at the front. The remaining teeth have somewhere to go. The case unfolds from back to front, into space that already exists, rather than trying to create and use space simultaneously.

The consequence that surprises clinicians encountering this for the first time: **IPR is rarely needed.** When distalization works predictably — as it does in the discluded environment of a functionally planned case — the arch has the space it requires. The teeth align into it. No enamel reduction required. This is not a universal rule. But the reflexive use of IPR as a crowding-relief tool in every moderately crowded case disappears when distalization is treated as a reliable first-phase movement.

*Section 5: Six questions for a functional setup review*

When the plan arrives, the functional clinician asks different questions from the conventional one:

1. Are the interceptors bilateral and symmetric at every stage?
2. Are the interceptors within the correct inclination range throughout the sequence?
3. Is disclusion maintained consistently, or do posterior teeth prematurely re-contact at certain stages?
4. Does the distalization phase complete before the anterior alignment movements begin?
5. Is there a point in the sequence where a new registration would be appropriate before the next phase?
6. Does the planned final occlusion show bilateral simultaneous contacts at the therapeutic position, with anterior guidance functioning and no posterior excursive interferences?

These are the questions that determine whether the plan is genuinely functional or merely well-labelled.

Each question as a check node with Yes/Confirmed → proceed and No/Uncertain → specific action. Visual style consistent with Ch 9 and Ch 10 decision trees. Caption: "Approving a functional setup requires different questions from approving a conventional one. A plan where the teeth look correct but the interceptors are inclined beyond their geometric limit will not deliver what was planned."]*

---

> **COMMON MISCONCEPTION — Chapter 11**
>
> *"If the plan shows good final tooth positions, the functional elements are probably correct too."*
>
> The final tooth position is the last thing the functional review should check. What matters first is whether the interceptors are geometrically valid across the full treatment sequence — because a beautifully positioned final arch on a setup with inclined interceptors at stage 12 will never reach that final arch. The teeth will be moving in an environment the plan did not design for, from a jaw position the interceptors stopped controlling three months into treatment. Review the sequence first. Review the endpoint last.

> **CHAPTER 11 IN 60 SECONDS**
>
> - Occlusal interceptors are the mechanism that holds the mandible in the therapeutic position at every stage of treatment — they are not attachments, not artefacts, and not errors
> - Posterior disclusion through interceptors removes occlusal load from the movement environment, making distalization, torque correction, and vertical movements significantly more predictable

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: The first treatment appointment*

The first active treatment appointment deserves more deliberateness than it usually receives. This is not an administrative visit — it is the first time the patient experiences the treatment physically, and it sets the tone for every review that follows.

**Attachments.** In most functional aligner cases, attachments are placed at the first appointment. The bonding technique is identical to conventional practice. What is specific to the functional case is the attachment design — the shapes and positions selected by the functional technician to support the interceptor mechanics and the planned movement sequence. The clinician should be able to explain why the attachments are positioned as they are, because patients ask.

**IPR — rarely, deliberately.** As established in Chapter 11, functional aligner cases that begin with distalization rarely require IPR. If IPR is indicated at the first appointment, it was specified in the plan for a specific reason — not as a default crowding management step.

**Three things worth saying to the patient at this appointment:**

First: what the treatment is actually doing. Not just "moving your teeth." The functional approach has a specific goal — a jaw relationship that is comfortable for the joints as well as aesthetically correct. The patient does not need the full anatomy lecture, but they should understand that this treatment was designed for their bite, not just their smile.

Second: what to expect. New stage tenderness is normal. The bite feeling different in the first days of a new aligner is normal. What is not normal — and what the patient should report immediately — is jaw pain, a new click, or a bite change that does not resolve within a few days of a new stage.

Third: how the review visits work. The patient is a monitoring partner. At every review, they will be asked directly about the bite feel. Their report is clinical data.

> *[INSIGHT BOX: "What to Say at Appointment One"*
>
> *"These aligners are designed to move your teeth into the position that is most comfortable for your jaw joints, not just the most aesthetic position. You might feel the bite shift as treatment progresses — that is intentional. Tell me at every visit if anything feels different, because your experience of the bite is information I use to manage the case. If you ever feel jaw pain that does not resolve within a couple of days of a new aligner, contact us before moving to the next stage."]*

*Section 2: What does not change*

Checking tracking, managing discomfort at new stage changes, handling patient communication, performing scheduled IPR, monitoring attachment integrity — none of this is different from the aligner treatment you already practise. The functional approach does not require you to unlearn your existing clinical skills.

The monitoring check adds two to three minutes per review visit. On most visits, nothing new is found. The value of the routine is not what it catches most of the time — it is what it catches on the visits where something has changed before the patient has registered it as a problem.

*Section 3: The monitoring rhythm*

A functional aligner review visit, for a Green patient in an uncomplicated phase, adds the following to a standard aligner review:

- **Tracking check framed as a functional question:** If the aligners do not fit as expected — is this mechanical failure (poor wear hours, inadequate seating) or positional change (the jaw relationship has shifted)?
- **Brief functional screen:** "Any jaw discomfort, morning tightness, or change in how the bite feels since the last visit?" If no — proceed. If yes — investigate.
- **Load test at defined intervals:** Not at every visit for Green patients. At every visit for Amber patients, and any time the patient reports jaw-related symptoms.

*Section 4: The fork in the road — mechanical vs. functional non-tracking*

This is the moment that the chapter title earns its irony. "Familiar territory" — until it isn't.

Non-tracking is familiar. You have seen it in every aligner case you have ever run. The standard response is equally familiar: take new records, plan a refinement, submit to the lab.

In a conventional aligner case, that response is correct. The non-tracking is mechanical — poor wear, inadequate seating, attachment failure, movement that the biomechanics did not deliver as planned. New records. Refinement. Move on.

In a functional aligner case, non-tracking can be mechanical or functional. Mechanical non-tracking looks the same as in any other aligner case: the teeth have not moved as planned, the aligner does not fit, the plan needs adjustment. Functional non-tracking looks identical on the surface — but the underlying cause is a changed jaw relationship. The mandible is no longer where the plan expected it to be. The interceptors, designed for the original CR, are now slightly off. The teeth are moving, but not in the environment the plan designed.

If you plan the refinement from the mechanical instinct — new records, straight to the lab — you plan the refinement from a jaw relationship that has changed. The refinement will be built on the same shifted foundation. It will not resolve the non-tracking.

The fork is this: before any refinement is planned, ask one question. **Is the functional baseline the same as it was at the start of treatment?**

Branch 1: functional baseline check → "Unchanged?" → Yes → mechanical non-tracking → standard refinement workflow. Branch 2: functional baseline check → "Changed?" → Yes → functional non-tracking → new CR registration required before refinement. Caption: "Non-tracking in a functional case has two possible causes. The response differs completely depending on which one you are dealing with."]*

*Section 5: Confirming the functional baseline — two minutes at the chair*

Confirming that the functional baseline has not changed takes two to three minutes:

- **Load test:** Negative, consistent with baseline?
- **Muscle palpation:** No new tenderness?
- **Patient report:** Bite stable, no morning symptoms?
- **Shimstock check:** Bilateral contacts at the therapeutic position?

All four consistent with the start of treatment: the functional baseline is unchanged. The non-tracking is mechanical. Standard refinement workflow.

Any finding changed from baseline: a new registration is indicated. Fifteen minutes. New brief. Refinement from the correct jaw relationship.

**Content Outline:**

*Section 1: Why aligned is not finished*

Aligner treatment is designed to move teeth. It does this well. When the final aligner is worn, the teeth are in the positions the plan specified. The photographs document an improvement. By the metrics the software used to measure success, the case is complete.

But the software's metrics are geometrical. They measure tooth position. They do not measure whether the jaw closes to bilateral contacts in the therapeutic position. They do not measure whether the anterior teeth are discluding the posterior teeth in excursion. They do not measure whether the condyles are seated and comfortable, whether the muscles are at rest, or whether the patient's bite will still look like this in two years.

*Section 2: The six criteria for functional finishing*

**Criterion 1: Bilateral simultaneous contacts at the therapeutic position.** At final occlusion in the CR position, the posterior teeth contact bilaterally and simultaneously. No premature unilateral contact. No deflective contact from CR to MIP. Tested with shimstock, bilaterally.

**Criterion 2: Anterior guidance functioning.** In excursive movements, the anterior teeth disclude the posterior teeth. In lateral excursion, the canine — or an anterior group — carries the guidance. In protrusion, the incisors disclude the posterior teeth. Tested directly with shimstock in excursion.

**Criterion 3: No posterior excursive interferences.** No posterior tooth contact in any excursive position. Tested with shimstock in lateral and protrusive movements. The virtual articulator detected potential interferences at setup — the clinical test confirms whether they have been resolved in the finished case.

**Criterion 4: Condylar stability confirmed.** Load test negative. Muscle palpation normal. No new tenderness in the masseters, temporalis, or medial pterygoids compared with pre-treatment baseline.

**Criterion 5: Patient-reported bite stability.** The patient reports that the bite feels settled, even, and comfortable. No morning tightness. No awareness of the bite changing between aligner stages. This is clinical data — not a satisfaction survey.

**Criterion 6: Occlusal plane maintained (or deliberately altered with documented justification).** The occlusal plane at debond is consistent with the planned endpoint — not the result of uncontrolled vertical movement during treatment.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 13.1: "The Six Criteria — Clinical Tests at Debond" — a structured two-column table. Left column: each criterion numbered and named. Right column: the clinical test that confirms it (shimstock / excursion check / load test / palpation / patient report / occlusal plane comparison). Visual style: clean clinical checklist. Caption: "Functional finishing is confirmed by clinical assessment, not photographs. These six tests take four minutes at debond."]*

*Section 3: What to do when the case is aligned but not functionally finished*

Most well-monitored functional aligner cases will satisfy all six criteria at the planned endpoint. When they do not, the pattern of which criteria are failing identifies the refinement needed.

**Unilateral or deflective contacts → interceptor refinement.** The jaw relationship needs adjustment, not just more tooth movement.

**Posterior excursive interference → targeted refinement.** One or two teeth are in the way of anterior guidance. A short targeted refinement is the most efficient solution.

**Canine guidance absent → orthodontic finishing.** The canines have not reached their planned positions. A refinement addresses their final positions specifically.

**Load test positive at debond → case should not be debonded.** A positive load test at planned finish indicates that the joint was not in a stable state at the treatment endpoint. This is not a refinement indication — it is a clinical pause. Re-evaluate the functional baseline before deciding whether to continue treatment or transition to a different modality.

*Section 4: Documentation at debond*

The debond record set for a functionally completed case should include:

- Shimstock findings at CR: bilateral / unilateral / deflective — documented
- Excursive contact check: anterior guidance present / interference identified and location
- Load test result: negative / positive — documented
- Muscle palpation: normal / tenderness — location documented
- Patient verbal report: recorded in notes
- Occlusal plane photographs: frontal and lateral at debond, compared with pre-treatment

Four minutes of documentation. The record that makes the case defensible in five years if someone asks why the bite is the way it is.

> *[ILLUSTRATION 13.2: "Functional Finishing — Decision Tree for Refinement" — branching diagram. Entry: final aligner worn. Six criterion nodes, each with Pass → confirmed / Fail → specific refinement action. Base: all six pass → debond. One fail → targeted refinement → recheck. Load test positive → clinical pause, reassess. Caption: "The six criteria determine whether to debond or refine. The pattern of failures tells you exactly what the refinement needs to address."]*

> **COMMON MISCONCEPTION — Chapter 16**
>
> *"If the evidence isn't strong, the clinical approach isn't valid."*
>
> Every clinical discipline practises in advance of complete evidence. The question is not whether evidence exists for every decision — it does not, in any branch of dentistry.
